Q3.  What  is  k-nearest  neighbor  classification?  How  does  it  differ  from  nearest  neighbor  classification? 
Describe a situation in which it might work better than nearest-neighbor classification. 
 
K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) classification is  a  type  of supervised learning algorithm used  for classification 
tasks. In KNN classification, the class of a data point is determined by the class of the majority of its k nearest 
neighbors in the feature space. KNN is a non-parametric algorithm, meaning it does not make any assumptions 
about the underlying data distribution. 
The primary difference between KNN and Nearest Neighbor lies in the number of neighbors considered. KNN 
considers the majority class among the k nearest neighbors, providing a more robust decision-making process 
by reducing the impact of noise or outliers. 
Situations where KNN might work better than Nearest Neighbor Classification: 
1.   Noise Reduction: 
•     If the dataset contains outliers or noisy data points, using KNN with a higher value of k (e.g., 
k=5 or k=10) can help reduce the impact of individual outliers. Nearest Neighbor, with k=1, 
might be more susceptible to the influence of noise. 
2.   Smoothing Effect: 
•     KNN with larger k values  introduces a smoothing effect, making the model less sensitive to 
local fluctuations in the data. This can be beneficial when dealing with datasets where there is 
variability in the class labels of nearby points. 
3.   Improved Generalization: 
•     KNN with an appropriate value of k can provide better generalization to unseen data compared 
to  Nearest  Neighbor.  A  balanced  choice  of  k  helps  in  making  decisions  based  on  a  more 
representative neighborhood rather than relying on a single nearest neighbor.

beneficial  to  preprocess  images  by  smoothing  to  enhance  the  effectiveness  of  subsequent 
algorithms.  
•     Usefulness:   Smoothing  can  help  simplify  image  structures  and  improve  the  performance  of 
algorithms by removing unnecessary details.

Q5. What are some ways that matching is employed in computer vision, and why is it often a hard problem? 
 
Matching plays a crucial role in various tasks in computer vision, where the goal is to find  correspondences 
between objects, features, or patterns in different images. Some common ways that matching is employed in 
computer vision include: 
 
Feature  Matching:  Feature  matching  involves  identifying  and  matching  distinctive  keypoints  or  features 
between  images.  This  is  commonly  used  in  tasks  such  as  image  alignment,  object  recognition,  and  image 
stitching. 
 
Template Matching: Template matching involves comparing a template (a small image patch) with regions of 
an image to find occurrences of the template. It is often used for object detection and localization in images. 
 
Object Detection and Recognition: Matching is used to detect and recognize objects in images by comparing 
them to known object models or templates. This includes tasks such as face recognition, object tracking, and 
scene understanding. 
 
Stereo Matching: Stereo matching involves finding corresponding points in stereo image pairs captured from 
different viewpoints. It is used to reconstruct 3D depth information and generate depth maps for tasks such as 
3D scene reconstruction and depth estimation. 
 
Shape Matching: Shape matching involves comparing the shapes of objects in images to determine similarities 
or  differences.  It  is  used  in  tasks  such  as  shape  recognition,  shape-based  object  detection,  and  contour 
matching. 
 
Q6. What are the advantages of using homogeneous coordinates in image transformation? Give an example. 
 
Homogeneous coordinates offer several advantages in image transformation tasks,  particularly in computer 
graphics and computer vision. Some of these advantages include: 
Uniform Representation: Homogeneous coordinates provide a unified representation for points, vectors, and 
transformations  in  a  projective  space.  This  simplifies  mathematical  operations  and  algorithms  by  treating 
translations, rotations, scaling, and perspective transformations uniformly. 
Simplifies Translation: In Cartesian coordinates, translation involves adding fixed coordinates to every point, 
which can be cumbersome and inefficient. Homogeneous coordinates allow translation to be represented as 
matrix multiplication, simplifying the math and making it more consistent with other transformations.

Q7. Why is a Gaussian filter preferred to a box filter? 
 
A box filter often leads to image artifacts called ringing. The Gaussian filter does not do that. 
Smoothing Effect: A Gaussian filter provides a smoother and more natural-looking result compared to a box 
filter.  This  is  because  the  Gaussian  filter  applies  a  weighted  average  to  neighboring  pixels  based  on  their 
distance from the center, resulting in a more gradual and visually pleasing blur. In contrast, a box filter applies 
a uniform average to neighboring pixels, which can lead to abrupt transitions and noticeable artifacts in the 
smoothed image.

detector and SIFT algorithm. 
 
(a) Corner detection is a fundamental technique in computer vision used to identify and localize corners or 
interest points in an image. These corners typically represent significant changes in intensity or gradients and 
are often associated with edges, corners, or junctions of objects in the scene. Here's a brief overview of how 
corner detection can be performed in an image: 
1.   Gradient Calculation: Compute the gradients of the image using gradient operators such as the Sobel 
or  Scharr  operators.  Gradients  highlight  regions  with  significant  intensity  changes,  which  often 
correspond to edges and corners. 
2.   Gradient Magnitude and Orientation: Calculate the magnitude and orientation of the gradients at 
each pixel. The magnitude represents the strength of the gradient, while the orientation indicates the 
direction of the gradient. 
3.   Structure Tensor: Compute the structure tensor for each pixel in the image. The structure tensor is a 
matrix that summarizes the local gradient information around each pixel and is used to characterize 
the local structure of the image. 
4.   Corner Response Function: Use the structure tensor to compute a corner response function, which 
measures the likelihood of a pixel being part of a corner. Common corner response functions include 
the Harris corner detector and the Shi-Tomasi corner detector. These functions typically consider the 
eigenvalues of the structure tensor to quantify cornerness. 
5.   Thresholding: Apply a threshold to the corner response function to identify pixels with sufficiently 
high  corner  scores.  This  thresholding  step  helps  distinguish  corners  from  noise  and  low-contrast

The Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) algorithm is a widely used method for detecting and describing 
local features in images. Here are the main steps of the SIFT algorithm: 
1.   Scale-space Extrema Detection: 
•     Construct a scale-space representation of the input image by applying Gaussian blurring and 
downsampling (octave decomposition). 
•     Detect potential interest points or keypoints at different scales by searching for local extrema 
in the scale-space space. 
2.   Keypoint Localization: 
•     Refine the location and scale of keypoints to sub -pixel accuracy using the Taylor expansion 
of the scale-space function. 
•     Eliminate  keypoints  with  low  contrast  or  poorly  localized  keypoints  by  comparing  their 
intensity with neighboring pixels and applying a threshold. 
3.   Orientation Assignment: 
•     Assign an orientation to each keypoint based on the local gradient directions computed from 
the image gradients. 
•     Construct a histogram of gradient orientations in the local neighborhood of each keypoint. 
•     Select the dominant orientation(s) from the histogram as the orientation(s) of the keypoint. 
4.   Descriptor Calculation: 
•     Compute a descriptor for each keypoint to describe its appearance and local image structure. 
•     Define a local image patch (e.g., 16x16 or 8x8 pixels) around each keypoint location. 
•     Compute gradient magnitude and orientation for each pixel in the patch. 
•     Construct  a  histogram  of  gradient  orientations  within  the  patch,  weighted  by  gradient 
magnitude. 
•     Normalize the histogram to ensure invariance to changes in illumination and contrast. 
•     Concatenate the normalized histograms to form the final descriptor vector for the keypoint. 
5.   Keypoint Matching (Optional): 
•     Match keypoints between different images by comparing their descriptors. 
•     Use distance metrics such as Euclidean distance or normalized cross  -correlation to measure 
the similarity between descriptors. 
•     Apply  nearest-neighbor  matching  or  a  more  sophisticated  matching  strategy  (e.g.,  nearest-
neighbor ratio test) to establish correspondences between keypoints in different images.

appearance  in  a  scale-invariant  and  robust  manner,  making  it  widely  used  in  various  computer  vision 
applications such as object recognition, image stitching, and 3D reconstruction.

Machine learning can be  broadly categorized into three main categories: supervised learning, unsupervised 
learning, and reinforcement learning. Here's a description of each category along with their subtypes, example 
algorithms, and applications: 
 
Supervised Learning: 
Description: In supervised learning, the algorithm learns from labeled data, where each example in the training 
dataset is associated with a corresponding target or label. The goal is to learn a mapping from input features to 
output labels. 
Subtypes: 
Classification: The output variable is categorical, and the algorithm learns to classify input data into predefined 
classes or categories. 
Regression: The output variable is continuous, and the algorithm learns to predict a numerical value based on 
input features. 
 
Example Algorithms: 
Classification: Support Vector Machines (SVM), Decision Trees, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, Neural 
Networks. 
Regression:  Linear  Regression,  Ridge  Regression,  Lasso  Regression,  Support  Vector  Regression  (SVR), 
Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM). 
 
Applications: 
Classification:  Email  spam  detection,  sentiment  analysis,  image  classification,  medical  diagnosis,  fraud 
detection. 
Regression: Stock price prediction, housing price prediction, demand forecasting, weather forecasting, sales 
forecasting. 
 
Unsupervised Learning: 
Description:  In  unsupervised  learning,  the  algorithm  learns  from  unlabeled  data,  where  there  are  no  target 
labels associated with the input examples. The goal is to uncover hidden patterns, structures, or relationships 
in the data. 
 
Subtypes: 
Clustering: The algorithm groups similar data points together into clusters or segments based on their inherent 
characteristics or features. 
Dimensionality  Reduction:  The  algorithm  reduces  the  number  of  input  features  by  extracting  the  most 
important features or representations from the data. 
 
Example Algorithms: 
Clustering: K-means clustering, Hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM). 
Dimensionality   Reduction:   Principal   Component   Analysis   (PCA),   t-distributed   Stochastic   Neighbor 
Embedding (t-SNE), Autoencoders. 
Applications: 
Clustering: Customer segmentation, market basket analysis, image segmentation, anomaly detection. 
Dimensionality Reduction: Visualization of high -dimensional data, feature extraction for downstream tasks, 
compression of data. 
 
Reinforcement Learning: 
Description: In reinforcement learning, the algorithm learns to make sequential decisions by interacting with 
an environment. The algorithm receives feedback in the form of rewards or penalties based on its actions, and 
the goal is to learn a policy that maximizes cumulative rewards over time. 
 
Subtypes: 
Model-based RL: The algorithm learns an explicit model of the environment dynamics (transition probabilities 
and rewards) and uses it to make decisions. 
Model-free RL: The algorithm learns a policy or value function directly from interactions with the environment 
without explicitly modeling the environment dynamics.

Q15. What are intrinsic camera parameters? 
Intrinsic  camera  parameters  are  a  set  of  parameters  that  describe  the  internal  geometric  properties  and 
characteristics of a camera system. These parameters define how the camera projects a 3D scene onto a 2D 
image plane. Intrinsic parameters remain constant for a given camera and are typically determined during the 
camera calibration process. The main intrinsic camera parameters include: 
Focal Length (fx, fy): The focal length is the distance between the camera's optical center (or principal point) 
and the image plane, measured in millimeters. It determines the magnification of the camera and affects the 
field of view and perspective distortion in the captured   images. 
Principal Point (cx, cy): The principal point is the image coordinates (in pixels) of the camera's optical center, 
where the optical axis intersects the image plane. It represents the center of the image and affects the alignment 
of the image coordinates with the physical world.  
Skew ( s): The skew parameter accounts for any non -orthogonality between the image axes (x and y) due to 
manufacturing  imperfections  or  misalignment  in  the  camera  system. It  is  typically  close  to  zero  for  most 
cameras and is often ignored in practice.

Q18. What is the purpose of a histogram equalization in image processing? 
Histogram  equalization  is  a  technique  used  in  image  processing  to  enhance  contrast  by  redistributing  the 
intensity  values  of  an  image.  It  works  by  computing  the  cumulative  distribution  function  (CDF)  of  pixel 
intensities and mapping the original values to  a more uniform distribution, effectively spreading out frequent 
intensity levels. This improves visibility in images with poor contrast, making details in dark or bright regions 
more distinguishable.  
In  image  processing,  histogram  equalization  adjusts  the  intensity  distribution  of  an  image  to  enhance  its 
contrast. It works by redistributing pixel intensities so that they occupy the full available range, making dark 
regions brighter and bright regions d arker if necessary. This process flattens the histogram, ensuring a more 
uniform distribution of intensity values. The importance of this  lies in improving image  quality by making 
features more distinguishable, reducing loss of detail in underexposed or ov  erexposed areas, and enhancing 
the  dynamic  range.  It  is  particularly  useful  in  preprocessing  steps  for  tasks  like  edge  detection,  object 
recognition, and segmentation, where well-balanced contrast improves accuracy. 
The method is widely used in applications such as medical imaging, remote sensing, and facial recognition, 
where  enhanced  contrast  helps  in  feature  extraction  and  analysis,  ensuring  better  visual  clarity  and 
normalization across varying lighting conditions.
